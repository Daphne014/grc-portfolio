## The Big Idea

Most people hear "compliance" and think paperwork, audits, and stress. The session reframed that immediately: compliance is about trust, accountability, and reducing risk. It is not a one-time project, not purely an IT responsibility, and not a guarantee against breaches. It is an ongoing process built around accountability, not perfection.

---

## What Is a Cybersecurity Program?

A structured way an organization identifies, manages, and monitors cyber risk over time. The typical components are:

- Policies and procedures
- Risk assessments
- Training and awareness
- Incident response plans
- Vendor and third-party risk processes

Entry-level roles exist specifically to support and maintain these programs. That was a recurring theme throughout the session.

---

## Frameworks: What They Are and Who Makes Them

A framework is a structured guide that helps organizations build a cybersecurity program without prescribing exactly how to do it. The analogy used in class: a recipe tells you what ingredients you need, not which brand to buy.

Frameworks are created by governments, standards bodies, and industry groups. Critically, they are usually **voluntary**. Enforcement comes from business reality: customers, insurers, regulators, and contracts.

### Key Frameworks Covered

| Framework | Who Created It | Best For | Certification? |
|---|---|---|---|
| NIST CSF | U.S. Government (NIST) | Risk management, North American orgs | No |
| ISO/IEC 27001 | ISO + IEC (international) | Global operations, enterprise clients | Yes |
| CyberSecure Canada | Canadian Government (CCCS) | Canadian SMEs | Yes |

**NIST CSF** organizes security around five functions: Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. It is widely used in GRC and analyst roles as a common language for risk.

**ISO/IEC 27001** is the most recognized international standard for information security management. It is often required in contracts and carries strong credibility with global partners.

**CyberSecure Canada** is government-backed, designed specifically for Canadian small and mid-sized businesses, and increasingly recognized by insurers and partners operating in Canada.

## Canadian Privacy Laws: Mandatory, Not Optional

The session drew a clear line between frameworks (guidance) and laws (mandatory).

**PIPEDA** (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act) is Canada's federal private-sector privacy law. It sets rules for how organizations collect, use, share, and protect personal information. Key obligations: consent, purpose limitation, safeguards, and breach notification. Enforced by the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada.

**Law 25** (Quebec) is a strong provincial law that significantly modernized Quebec's privacy framework. It is similar in spirit to GDPR and raises expectations for privacy governance beyond Quebec's borders.

Non-compliance consequences: fines, mandatory disclosures, and reputational damage.

## How to Choose the Right Framework

Five questions to ask about any organization:

1. What country do they operate in?
2. What type of data do they handle?
3. Who are their customers?
4. Are they regulated?
5. Do contracts require proof of security?

Examples from the session:
- Canadian nonprofit → CyberSecure Canada + PIPEDA
- SaaS company with global clients → ISO 27001 + NIST CSF
- Healthcare-adjacent org → NIST CSF + privacy law alignment

## Entry-Level Roles That Use This Knowledge

- GRC Analyst (Junior)
- Compliance Assistant / Coordinator
- Privacy Analyst (Junior)
- Cyber Program Coordinator
- Risk & Controls Analyst (Entry-Level)

Day-to-day tasks in these roles include mapping controls to frameworks, supporting risk assessments, maintaining compliance documentation, assisting with audits, and explaining requirements to non-technical teams.

The key message from this session: compliance roles reward clarity, organization, and communication, not deep technical skills.
